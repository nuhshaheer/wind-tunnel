# Dimension sheet: contraction (drawing item 30)

**Status: the inside shape ([D-022](../../DECISIONS.md)) and the split ([D-023](../../DECISIONS.md)) are Accepted
(owner, 2026-11-26); ready to draw. The flanges and joints are Proposed ([flange sheet](flanges.md)); hole sizes come
from the [test coupons](coupons.md).** Generated by `python cad/sizing/contraction.py`, which also
writes the two points files. All dimensions are in mm.

## What it does

The contraction speeds the air up 6.25 times between the settling chamber and the test section. That evens out the
speed across the section and shrinks the turbulence left by the screens. Its inside wall must be smooth and free of
steps, especially near the exit, where the air is fastest and the boundary layer thinnest (D-005).

### Points files

- [`contraction_wall_fusion_cm.csv`](contraction_wall_fusion_cm.csv): 51 points every 5 mm, `x,y,z` with no header,
  **in centimetres**. Fusion 360's sample script *ImportSplineCSV* reads the numbers as centimetres (Fusion's internal
  unit; Autodesk forum), so a file in mm would come in 10 times too big. x runs along the flow, y is the half-width,
  z = 0.
- [`contraction_wall_mm.csv`](contraction_wall_mm.csv): the same points in mm with a header, the inside side, the wall
  angle and the speed ratio, for reading.

A spline through these points matches the equation to about 0.001 mm (our estimate).

### Drawing it in Fusion 360 (suggestions)

**The wall curve is exactly a degree-5 control point spline (agent, 2026-11-26).** Fusion has no equation-curve tool,
but this curve does not need one. The polynomial 6s⁵ − 15s⁴ + 10s³ is a Bézier curve whose six control points are
evenly spaced along the flow: three at the inlet half-width and three at the exit's. `python cad/sizing/contraction.py`
prints them and checks them against the equation: 2.2 × 10⁻¹³ mm apart over 2001 points, i.e. identical.

| Control point | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| x (mm) | 0 | 50 | 100 | 150 | 200 | 250 |
| y, half-width (mm) | 125 | 125 | 125 | 50 | 50 | 50 |

**Option A (recommended): a control point spline driven by parameters.** Nothing to import, exact, and it follows the
parameters if a size ever changes.
1. Sketch → Create → Spline → **Control Point Spline**, degree **5** (set it in the command dialog), six points.
2. Constrain the control polygon: points 0–1–2 on one horizontal line at `sc_in/2`, points 3–4–5 on one at `ts_in/2`,
   each horizontal gap `con_len/5` (parameters in [README.md](README.md)). The ends come out with zero slope and
   zero curvature by themselves, so no tangent constraints are needed.
3. **Check it:** a vertical construction line at x = 50 must cut the curve at y = 120.656, and one at x = 125 at
   y = 87.500 (Inspect → Measure). If not, the spline's degree is not 5; say so before going on.

**Option B: import the points file.**
1. Run the sample script: Utilities → Add-Ins → Scripts and Add-Ins (Shift+S) → *ImportSplineCSV*, and pick the cm
   file. It makes a sketch on the XY plane.
2. **Check the import before using it:** the spline must start at (0, 125), pass (125, 87.5) and end at (250, 50) in mm.
   If it is 10 times too big, the script version treats numbers differently: say so and the agent makes a mm file.
3. Add horizontal tangent constraints at both ends of the spline, so the wall meets the neighbouring walls flat.

Then, either way:

4. **An exact square duct from one curve:** mirror the curve about the axis to get the closed profile between +y and −y.
   Extrude it as a solid across the flow, then extrude the same profile turned 90° about the flow axis, and intersect
   the two bodies. The result is the air volume, square at every x. Shell it or subtract it from a larger body to get the
   3 mm wall. A loft between two squares only approximates the curve.

### Why it is split at x = 82

The inlet end cannot print in one piece: 250 inside plus two 3 mm walls is 256, the whole bed, before any flange. A
one-piece downstream part can start where its upstream flange fits the bed with about 3 mm to spare on each side:

| Flange width beyond the wall | Joint at x | Inside there | One-piece part's length | Air speed there | q there |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 76.9 | 224.0 | 173.1 | 0.20 × test section | 4.0 % |
| **12 (chosen flange)** | **82** | **219.7** | **168** | 0.21 × | 4.3 % |
| 14 | 86.1 | 216.0 | 163.9 | 0.21 × | 4.6 % |

- The joint sits where the air moves at about a fifth of the test-section speed, so a small step there matters far less
  than near the exit (Morel1975: contractions with large ratios tolerate irregularities at the slow end).
- **Printing the one-piece part:** stand it on its exit (screw) flange. The walls lean outward going up, at most 29.4°
  from vertical, so the inside needs no supports. **Corrected 2026-11-27:** the flange at the top (the glued joint) needs
  slicer supports (tree supports from the build plate, under that flange only), not the 45° chamfer planned here. The
  wall under it leans inward going down, so a 45° chamfer would run about 29 mm down before meeting it: about 250 g of
  PLA (estimate).
- **The inlet ring:** four corner pieces, each with a curved wall from the points file, printed and glued as in the
  flange sheet.

## Finishing

- Each wall is straight across the flow and curved only along it. **A flat sanding block held across the flow fits the
  wall exactly**, so sand across the flow (220 → 600 grit, wet; on hand). A flat block held along the flow would flatten
  the bends (radius 158).
- Printed standing, the 29° slope leaves layer ridges about 0.1 mm high (estimate at 0.2 mm layers). Sanding removes
  them; finer layers in the steep middle, or the filler primer (optional, buy list §2), reduce the sanding.
